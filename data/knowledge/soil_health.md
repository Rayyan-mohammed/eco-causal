# Soil Health

## Soil organic carbon and soil biology
Soil organic carbon (SOC) is the primary energy source for soil microbial communities. Soils with higher SOC content consistently support greater microbial biomass and diversity, because organic matter provides the carbon substrate that bacteria and fungi metabolize. Depleted SOC leaves soil biology energy-starved regardless of other inputs.
Source: Lori et al. (2017), "Organic farming enhances soil microbial abundance and activity: a meta-analysis and meta-regression", PLOS ONE 12(7):e0180442, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0180442; Tsiafouli et al. (2015), "Intensive agriculture reduces soil biodiversity across Europe", Global Change Biology 21(2):973-985, https://doi.org/10.1111/gcb.12752.

## Cover crops and organic carbon
Cover crops planted between main cropping cycles add root biomass and exudates to the soil, increasing organic carbon over time. This effect is well documented in temperate and adequately watered systems. In water-limited systems, however, the additional plant growth competes for scarce soil moisture during establishment, which can reduce the following cash crop's yield even as it improves organic carbon. Below roughly 400mm of annual rainfall, this water-competition tradeoff becomes the dominant effect and cover cropping should be evaluated carefully against moisture-conserving alternatives such as agroforestry.
Source: Poeplau & Don (2015), Agriculture, Ecosystems & Environment 200:33-41, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167880914004873; Blanco-Canqui et al. (2015), Agronomy Journal 107:2449-2474, https://doi.org/10.2134/agronj15.0086; FAO Global Soil Partnership, https://www.fao.org/global-soil-partnership/en (dryland cover cropping guidance).

## Tillage and organic carbon loss
Conventional tillage physically breaks up soil aggregates and exposes previously protected organic matter to microbial oxidation, accelerating the loss of stored carbon. Reduced or no-till systems slow this loss and, combined with residue retention, can rebuild soil carbon over multiple seasons.
Source: FAO Conservation Agriculture programme, https://www.fao.org/conservation-agriculture/en/; Six, Bossuyt, Degryze & Denef (2004), Soil & Tillage Research 79:7-31, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.still.2004.03.008.

## Soil erosion and topsoil carbon
Erosion by wind and water preferentially removes the carbon-rich topsoil layer, since organic matter concentrates near the surface. Bare or recently tilled soil is far more vulnerable to erosion than soil with vegetative cover, and erosion losses of soil organic carbon can outweigh gains from other management practices if ground cover is not maintained.
Source: FAO (2019), Soil Erosion: The Greatest Challenge for Sustainable Soil Management, https://openknowledge.fao.org/items/6c070e1e-6533-4b7e-ba5f-a2f21a0e59ff.

## Soil aggregate structure
Stable soil aggregates form when microbial exudates, fungal hyphae, and earthworm activity bind mineral particles together. Good aggregate structure increases pore space, which in turn improves water infiltration and moisture retention. Earthworm burrowing is a particularly strong contributor to macropore formation.
Source: Six et al. (2004), https://doi.org/10.1016/j.still.2004.03.008; Edwards & Bohlen (1996), Biology and Ecology of Earthworms, 3rd ed., Chapman & Hall, ISBN 978-0-412-56160-3.

## Soil pH and earthworm populations
Soil pH governs nutrient availability and habitat suitability for soil organisms. Strongly acidic soils, generally below pH 5.5, are physiologically unfavorable for most earthworm species, reducing their abundance and the structural benefits they provide. Repeated high-rate application of ammonium-based nitrogen fertilizer is a common driver of soil acidification over multiple growing seasons.
Source: Edwards & Bohlen (1996), Biology and Ecology of Earthworms, 3rd ed., Chapman & Hall, ISBN 978-0-412-56160-3; FAO Global Soil Partnership, https://www.fao.org/global-soil-partnership/en (a single dedicated 'soil acidification' guidance note was not independently located in this pass).

## Soil salinity
Irrigation in poorly drained or arid and semi-arid conditions leaves dissolved salts behind as water evaporates, gradually raising soil salinity. Elevated salinity impairs crop root water uptake through osmotic stress and is also stressful for most soil microorganisms, reducing microbial diversity alongside yield.
Source: FAO Global Soil Partnership, International Network on Salt-Affected Soils (INSAS), https://www.fao.org/global-soil-partnership/insas/en.

## Moisture retention practices can reduce surface salt accumulation
Practices that raise soil moisture retention, such as mulching, suppress surface evaporation. Since evaporation is what drives the upward capillary movement that concentrates salts at the soil surface, reducing it also reduces surface salt buildup — an effect documented specifically under irrigated, evaporation-driven salinization. This is a narrower claim than a general "moisture always reduces salinity" rule; the long-term interaction between mulching, irrigation practice, and salinity is more complex than this single mechanism.
Source: Hou, Zhu & Jin (2016), "Surface Drainage and Mulching Drip-Irrigated Tomatoes Reduces Soil Salinity and Improves Fruit Yield", PLOS ONE, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0154799.

## Nitrogen availability and yield
Nitrogen is typically the most yield-limiting nutrient in cropping systems, and yield increases with nitrogen availability up to a crop-specific optimal rate. Beyond that threshold, additional nitrogen provides little further yield benefit and instead increases the risk of leaching and runoff into nearby water bodies.
Source: FAO Global Soil Partnership, https://www.fao.org/global-soil-partnership/en (general soil fertility and nutrient management resources; a single dedicated 'soil fertility guidance' document was not independently located in this pass).

## Tillage's direct physical effects on soil structure and earthworms
Beyond its effect on organic carbon, tillage mechanically disrupts soil in two more immediate ways: it directly breaks up soil aggregates through physical force, independent of any carbon-oxidation pathway, and it directly kills and injures earthworms while destroying their burrows. Both effects show up faster than the slower, carbon-mediated structural decline.
Source: Six, Bossuyt, Degryze & Denef (2004), Soil & Tillage Research 79:7-31, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.still.2004.03.008; Edwards & Bohlen (1996), Biology and Ecology of Earthworms, 3rd ed., Chapman & Hall, ISBN 978-0-412-56160-3.

## Crop diversity's direct effect on soil carbon and microbial life
Diversified rotations, particularly those including legumes, add more varied organic inputs and root architectures to soil than a single repeated crop does, building organic carbon directly rather than only through reduced degradation. The same varied root exudates and residue types also feed a more diverse soil microbial community than monoculture supports.
Source: FAO (2019), The State of the World's Biodiversity for Food and Agriculture, https://openknowledge.fao.org/items/b355c300-72ed-4a63-be07-8295c80ec7f1; Tsiafouli et al. (2015), Global Change Biology 21(2):973-985, https://doi.org/10.1111/gcb.12752.

## Overgrazing's direct effect on soil carbon
Beyond compacting soil, grazing pressure that exceeds land carrying capacity removes vegetation faster than it regenerates, directly reducing the organic matter returned to soil and increasing the erosion losses that strip away what carbon remains.
Source: FAO, Sustainable rangeland management in Sub-Saharan Africa: Guidelines to Good Practice, https://www.fao.org/family-farming/detail/en/c/1632938/.
