# Soil Health

## Soil organic carbon and soil biology
Soil organic carbon (SOC) is the primary energy source for soil microbial communities. Soils with higher SOC content consistently support greater microbial biomass and diversity, because organic matter provides the carbon substrate that bacteria and fungi metabolize. Depleted SOC leaves soil biology energy-starved regardless of other inputs.
Source: Lori et al. (2017), "Organic farming enhances soil microbial abundance and activity: a meta-analysis and meta-regression", PLOS ONE; Tsiafouli et al. (2015), Global Change Biology.

## Cover crops and organic carbon
Cover crops planted between main cropping cycles add root biomass and exudates to the soil, increasing organic carbon over time. This effect is well documented in temperate and adequately watered systems. In water-limited systems, however, the additional plant growth competes for scarce soil moisture during establishment, which can reduce the following cash crop's yield even as it improves organic carbon. Below roughly 400mm of annual rainfall, this water-competition tradeoff becomes the dominant effect and cover cropping should be evaluated carefully against moisture-conserving alternatives such as agroforestry.
Source: Poeplau & Don (2015), Agriculture, Ecosystems & Environment; Blanco-Canqui et al. (2015), Agronomy Journal; FAO Global Soil Partnership guidance on cover cropping in dryland systems.

## Tillage and organic carbon loss
Conventional tillage physically breaks up soil aggregates and exposes previously protected organic matter to microbial oxidation, accelerating the loss of stored carbon. Reduced or no-till systems slow this loss and, combined with residue retention, can rebuild soil carbon over multiple seasons.
Source: FAO (2011), Conservation Agriculture guidance; Six, Bossuyt, Degryze & Denef (2004), Soil & Tillage Research.

## Soil erosion and topsoil carbon
Erosion by wind and water preferentially removes the carbon-rich topsoil layer, since organic matter concentrates near the surface. Bare or recently tilled soil is far more vulnerable to erosion than soil with vegetative cover, and erosion losses of soil organic carbon can outweigh gains from other management practices if ground cover is not maintained.
Source: FAO (2019), Soil Erosion: The Greatest Challenge to Sustainable Soil Management.

## Soil aggregate structure
Stable soil aggregates form when microbial exudates, fungal hyphae, and earthworm activity bind mineral particles together. Good aggregate structure increases pore space, which in turn improves water infiltration and moisture retention. Earthworm burrowing is a particularly strong contributor to macropore formation.
Source: Six et al. (2004); Edwards & Bohlen (1996), Biology and Ecology of Earthworms.

## Soil pH and earthworm populations
Soil pH governs nutrient availability and habitat suitability for soil organisms. Strongly acidic soils, generally below pH 5.5, are physiologically unfavorable for most earthworm species, reducing their abundance and the structural benefits they provide. Repeated high-rate application of ammonium-based nitrogen fertilizer is a common driver of soil acidification over multiple growing seasons.
Source: Edwards & Bohlen (1996); FAO Global Soil Partnership, Soil Acidification guidance note.

## Soil salinity
Irrigation in poorly drained or arid and semi-arid conditions leaves dissolved salts behind as water evaporates, gradually raising soil salinity. Elevated salinity impairs crop root water uptake through osmotic stress and is also stressful for most soil microorganisms, reducing microbial diversity alongside yield.
Source: FAO Global Soil Partnership, Soil Salinization guidance.

## Nitrogen availability and yield
Nitrogen is typically the most yield-limiting nutrient in cropping systems, and yield increases with nitrogen availability up to a crop-specific optimal rate. Beyond that threshold, additional nitrogen provides little further yield benefit and instead increases the risk of leaching and runoff into nearby water bodies.
Source: FAO Global Soil Partnership, Soil Fertility guidance.
